forge · the home's worker hierarchy directions

Nine rows in web-home.html are the same row as every other row: a lead and its workers differ by nothing a person can see. The one signal the drawing carries, a 22px indent on the name, went out in web-home-spine.html and was not replaced. This sheet is the question stated properly: can you tell which row is the lead and which are its workers, at a glance, at 1600 and at 430, without reading a word of the row?

Every direction draws the same slice, and the slice is the home's own rows with the home's own stylesheet - the tokens, the five columns, the marks, every state colour. Only the treatment named under each heading differs, so what you are comparing is one mechanism at a time rather than seven redraws. The vocabulary is taken from tools that already solve this, and each heading names where its direction came from.

same row in every direction same states in every direction a lead is running, as is one worker no new token checked at 1600 and 430 nothing overflows

01 is the control and the only direction drawn without an indent: it is the treatment that replaced the drawing's indent and did not replace it with anything a person can see. 03 is the drawing, the indent on its own, and 02 is that indent plus a foot - Ved's direction - drawn beside 03 on purpose, so the difference between those two is the foot and nothing else. 04 to 07 are additions to the same indent, one thing each, so every direction is a change you can name rather than a redraw you have to compare whole.

02The spine with a footVed's direction, 2026-09-29 · Ant Design showLine · GNU tree

Ved, looking at 01: "take that spine and indent it inside like tree only, just an L shape rather than just a word. So it's L shape from logo, top level logo to the own row slope." And, on a first pass that read "indent it inside" as taking the indent away: "I am looking for an L shape, like a tree shape, extend the spines lower end where the logo is and move it inside through the indent so that it defines that it is a worker, it belongs to, there is a parent out there."

So the indent stays exactly as the drawing ships it, and the foot crosses it. The descent leaves the lead's own mark, runs down, and at each child turns once and reaches the worker's own row, its length the indent it crosses. That span is the message: a foot that stops short of the worker's row says nothing, which is what a first pass at 9px did. A tree's ├── crosses the whole indent by definition, which is why GNU tree 2.3.2, eza and lsd all spend 2.5 cells of line and a cell of space a level. The corner is square because Ant Design's showLine computes border-radius: 0 and the box-drawing glyphs carry no curve at all.

Worth knowing before anyone simplifies this back to a straight line: almost nothing modern draws an elbow. VS Code, GitHub's file tree, react-arborist, rc-tree, MUI X, Element Plus and JetBrains were each checked and none of them has a bend - a straight guide, or none. So this is a deliberate revival of the terminal's form rather than a web convention, and the arm and the square corner are the terminal's, taken from it rather than chosen. It is the one direction here whose vocabulary comes from outside the web.

--What is not drawn, and whymeasured on the control's slice

None of the seven reaches for weight, colour or fill, and the measurement is the reason rather than a preference: in this slice those three channels already belong to the state, and the row they make loudest is a worker.

measured the lead hub-modules and two of its workers, em-dash-sweep and steward, all compute to weight 600, colour --text and no background, so they are the same object in all three channels. job-hunt, a worker, computes to --warn on a warning fill, which makes the loudest row in the slice a worker rather than the lead.

A direction built on any of those three would spend a channel the page already spends on state, and it would sometimes rank a worker above its lead. That is why every direction here spends something the state vocabulary does not use: position, structure, or an element that is present on one row and absent on the other.

The same is true of the mark. A row's mark is its state and nothing else, so a shape reserved for leads would make a lead's state unreadable - which is why the caret below is drawn as a chevron and sits outside the mark column rather than becoming a second mark beside it.
